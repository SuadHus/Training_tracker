# How the elbows move in a barbell bench press

Research note, 2026-11-24. For whoever changes the lift animation, and for the lifter who wants to check it.

The grips: when this note was written the animation's hands were 0.67 m (bench) and 0.42 m (close grip) apart, and sections 1 to 5 compare the sources with those. The animation was changed with this note in hand. Its hands are now 0.76 and 0.50 m apart between their middles, which is 0.67 and 0.41 m between the forefingers; section 6.1 says why and what else changed.

Fact-checked 2026-11-24 against the sources themselves (M22, L21, L22, K17, N24, the McLaughlin tables as images, the IPF rulebook, the Nuckols, Rippetoe, Lewis, Feigenbaum and Kilgore texts, the PubMed abstracts). Numbers and statements that did not match their source were corrected; what could not be found is marked unverified.

This note uses only what three researchers found at a source. It adds no measurements. Where a number is plain arithmetic on reported numbers (a unit conversion, a subtraction), it says so. A cell that says "no source" has no source. Anything marked "unverified" is a number that was seen only in a search-tool snippet or a secondary page.

How to read the citation markers: each marker such as [M22] is a key to the list in section 7. Three kinds of evidence are mixed in the tables, and the markers tell them apart:

- Measured in a lab: [M22], [L21], [L22], [K17], [McL] (film study), [E89].
- Computer model of loads, not measured angles: [N24].
- Coaching text, an expert's description or recommendation without data: [Nu16], [Nu16b], [Nu15], [Ri11], [Ri18], [Le24], [Fe13], [Ki23]. The IPF rulebook [IPF26] is a rule, not a measurement.

Two things to know before reading anything else:

1. Nobody in the sources measured elbow flare through the descent. "Tuck on the way down" has no data behind it.
2. The two papers that give flare numbers use two different angles. [M22] measures the humerus against the sagittal plane of the thorax. [L21] measures a "shoulder abduction" whose definition is not in the text the researchers read, and it rises from about 42 to 58 deg at the chest to about 72 to 80 deg late in the ascent (chart reading). Do not mix them.

## 1. What the elbows do

Regular grip (hands about 0.67 m apart, the animation's grip when this note was written). Close grip afterwards.

1. At lockout the arms are vertical and straight. The bar is over the shoulder joint [Nu16][Ri11]. Nuckols says the elbows should face out to the sides at lockout ("elbows pointed out to the side", "screw your shoulders out") [Nu16].
2. On the way down the bar moves along a near-straight line, curved slightly concave toward the head, down and toward the feet [McL][Nu16b]. It falls 0.34 to 0.37 m in trained men at medium and wide grip [L21].
3. The elbows bend and drop. The rule minimum is the underside of the elbow level with or below the top of the shoulder joint [IPF26]. How far below lifters actually go is not reported.
4. How the elbow flare changes during the descent is not measured. Nuckols says it "will probably just take care of itself simply by touching the bar fairly low on your chest" (and describes tucking the elbows hard to cushion the descent only as a technique for very strong lifters) [Nu16]; Rippetoe's draft describes one humerus angle for the whole rep [Ri11].
5. At the chest the bar is about 13 cm toward the feet from the shoulder line: 13.4 cm in light experts, measured from the shoulder [McL Table 6]; 12.5 to 13.1 cm in [L21], whose column is headed "from start" (the reference point is assumed, section 2.1). The humerus is about 59 deg (medium grip) to 66 deg (wide grip) from the sagittal plane of the thorax, seen from the head end, and a few degrees behind the frontal plane of the shoulders [M22].
6. At the chest the elbow is bent about 87 deg (wide) to 101 deg (medium) [M22]. A second paper, with a different marker method, gives a more bent elbow [L21].
7. Coaches say the forearm is vertical at the chest [Ri11][Le24][Fe13][Ki23]. No paper measured the forearm angle at the chest from the side. The one frontal-plane measurement is a whole-rep mean: -0.8 deg (medium) and +12.5 deg (wide) [M22].
8. On the way up the bar goes toward the head right off the chest. World-class lifters of 1978 to 1980 (light experts) leave at about 60 deg to the horizontal (chest to first upward velocity peak); novices push almost straight up at about 84 deg [McL Table 7]. After that the experts' path keeps going up and toward the head at about 56 to 72 deg to the horizontal; it does not turn vertical (section 3).
9. Over the ascent the "abduction" angle of [L21] rises by about 27 deg (medium) and 22 deg (wide), chart reading, undefined plane. Coaches say to flare the elbows through the midrange so the bar comes back over the upper chest [Nu16]. No measured flare curve exists for the ascent either.
10. The sticking region ends about 0.96 s after the chest and at 46 to 55% of the bar's rise, with the elbow near 90 deg interior angle [L21].
11. Lockout again: bar over the shoulder, elbows locked, elbows facing out [Nu16][IPF26].

What the close grip (hands about 0.42 m, the animation's grip when this note was written) changes, nearest data 0.34 to 0.40 m:

- Less flare at the chest: 37.9 deg with elbows kept in, 56.5 deg when told to keep elbows out [M22]; about 42 deg [L21, chart].
- More elbow flexion at the chest: 110 to 114 deg [M22].
- The whole-rep frontal forearm mean has the opposite sign to the wide grip and is larger: -8.5 and -16.5 deg [M22]. The sign convention is not stated in the paper.
- A longer descent (0.399 m) [L21], a longer pre-sticking region (as a share of lift distance; the shares of duration did not differ) and a 23% higher peak velocity [K17]. The 0.43 m close-grip against 0.41 m preferred-grip lift distance in [K17] is not a significant difference at that paper's alpha of 0.01.
- A bigger rise in the chart abduction angle: about 30 deg [L21].

On that conversion the animation's regular grip, 0.67 m, lies midway between [M22] medium and wide, and its close grip, 0.42 m, lies next to [M22] narrow. A lifter with wider shoulders would hold the same percentages farther apart. The IPF limit is 81 cm between the forefingers [IPF26 4.2 item 5], so both animation grips are legal. In the regular-grip table, pairs are written "medium / wide". Which of the two the animation should follow is a judgement (section 6).

[M22] set its grips by the inner angle between the straight arm and the bar at lockout: 75 deg (medium), 65 deg (wide) and 85 deg (narrow). This sentence, the +/- values of the arch angle in 2.4 and the +/- values of the bar's travel in [K17] were added on 2026-11-24 from the researchers' working notes on the papers' own text and tables; they are not among the statements the fact-check listed.

Abbreviations: UBL = upper-body length (hip to shoulder), used by [McL]. "Toward the feet" is positive. The sign of [L21]'s horizontal column is assumed, not stated in the text the researchers read, and its reference point (the bar's own start or the shoulder) is not confirmed.

"Interior angle" means 180 deg is a straight arm, so elbow flexion is 180 minus the interior angle. [L21] reports interior angles. [M22] reports flexion.

[L21] chart values were read off a bar chart by the researcher, accuracy about plus or minus 2 deg, and are marked chart.

Events in [L21]: chest (v0); first velocity peak (vmax1, about 0.23 s after the chest, 3 to 5 cm up); first velocity minimum (vmin, end of the sticking region, about 0.96 s); second velocity peak (vmax2, 1.0 to 1.4 s, 31 to 37 cm up, which is 92 to 93% of the rise, arithmetic on Table 1). Heights are above the chest position.

## 3. The bar path

All from the side. "Toward the head" means back toward the shoulder line and beyond.

Down [McL, text copy and blog tables]:

- Light experts, novices and heavy experts all lower on nearly parallel, near-straight paths, concave toward the head [McL][Nu16b].
- Experts' lowering path sits about 10% UBL nearer the shoulders than novices'.
- Competition experts start at 95% of the way from hip to shoulder and touch at 70%, so the touch is 30% UBL short of the shoulder line. With UBL 0.466 m (light experts) that is about 14 cm. The researcher's arithmetic, not a figure in the book.
- Bar-to-shoulder distance at the chest, Table 6: light experts 5.27 in (13.4 cm), novices 7.44 in (18.9 cm), heavy experts 7.79 in (19.8 cm). Heavy experts' UBL is 0.508 m.
- Trained men in [L21] (1RM, touch-and-press): 0.125 to 0.131 m horizontal and 0.338 to 0.399 m vertical from the start to the chest, no grip difference in the horizontal part.
- Coaching: the bar descends 1 to 3 in (2.5 to 8 cm) along the sternum from the over-the-shoulder line to the touch [Ki23, citing Elliott 1989 and Kratiuk 2014, neither traced]. This does not match the 12 to 14 cm above and is not used for a target.

Up [McL]:

- Novices push nearly vertically first, then up and toward the head. They often raise the bar along a path lower on the chest than their lowering path.
- Experts do the opposite: a path with the opposite convexity, with a substantial horizontal component toward the head right off the chest. The paths converge near the end of the lift.
- Angle of displacement with the horizontal, Table 7 (read from the scan image): chest to first upward velocity peak 60.3 deg (light experts), 64.8 (heavy), 84.4 (novices). Peak to first velocity minimum 55.8, 62.0, 71.3. Minimum to end of lift 72.1, 66.1, 66.7 (no group difference marked). Each angle is a chord, not a path shape. 90 deg is vertical, so no expert segment is vertical. Experts and novices differ significantly (marked *) in the first two raising segments only.
- The push toward the head happens "before the bar is but a few inches up".
- The group difference in horizontal position grows fast, reaches 20% UBL at the sticking point and is 11% UBL at the finish. Experts finish 1 +/- 11% UBL beyond the shoulder.
- Heavy experts hold the bar farther from the shoulder than light experts, like novices early in the lift, then move it toward the shoulders through the ascent. [McL] ties this to the 81 cm grip limit and larger bodies.
- Experts' sticking point is closer to the shoulder; all sticking points are less than about 5 in above the chest.
- Elite lifters who kept improving pushed toward the head earlier each year (angles fell) [McL 2.7].
- Fatigued recreational lifters kept the bar "more directly over the shoulder" [DC07, abstract].

The term "J curve" is not used in the sources the researchers read. In plain words the sources describe: down and out toward the feet, then up and back toward the head from the first few centimetres, continuing up and toward the head to the finish. No source reports the bar first moving toward the feet after the touch. Elite lifters' horizontal travel is given only as UBL percentages and, for the raising phase, in inches (McL Table 6: light experts 13.4 cm from the shoulder at the chest, 4.4 cm at minimum bar velocity, so about 9 cm of travel toward the head over the first half of the ascent, arithmetic); the lowering travel of about 12 cm is arithmetic on UBL percentages. [L21] and [L22] measured recreationally trained men.

## 4. Timing and the sticking region

Descent:

- Competition lifters in [McL] (lowering from start to chest, Table 3 from the blog scan): novices 1.16 s, light experts 1.72 s, heavy experts 2.34 s. Peak downward acceleration of novices was 5 to 6 times the experts'.
- Coaching: about 2 to 3 s [Nu16]. Typical bar speed about 0.4 m/s; 3 to 5 s for a whole lift is common; competition pause about 2 s [McL].
- Lifters in [L21] lowered at a self-chosen speed of about 1 +/- 0.5 m/s in a touch-and-press protocol. This is not a competition descent. Do not mix the two.

Ascent:

- Raising time from the chest to the end, Table 3 from the blog scan: novices 1.76 s, light experts 2.21 s, heavy experts 1.91 s. The text copy of the book does not show this table, so these three numbers rest on one scan.
- First velocity peak about 0.23 s after the chest; end of the sticking region 0.96 to 0.97 s for all grips; second peak 1.03 s (narrow) to 1.37 s (wide) [L21].
- The sticking period began about 0.2 s after the upward movement began and lasted about 0.9 s [vdT10, abstract]. This matches [L21].
- Whole-lift duration, 27 resistance-trained men and women, 1RM: 3.47 +/- 1.43 s preferred wide grip vs 2.98 +/- 1.03 s close grip. Table 1 prints p = 0.044, but the paper's alpha is 0.01 and its text says there was no significant difference in duration, so treat the 0.5 s as not established. Peak velocity was 23% higher with the close grip (significant) [K17].

Where the sticking region is:

- Height: end of the sticking region at 46% (wide), 50% (medium), 55% (narrow) of the descent distance, arithmetic on [L21] Table 1 (0.154, 0.186, 0.219 m over 0.338, 0.373, 0.399 m). Narrow-grip velocity minimum higher above the chest is also stated by [K17] quoting [GT16]; not seen in [GT16].
- [McL]: every one of 48 lifters had a minimum of upward acceleration after leaving the chest. It lies less than about 5 in (12.7 cm, conversion) above the chest. The force dip is 14% (competition lifters) and 44% (novices). Experts exerted 87% more force at it.
- Coaching: most people miss with the bar stuck 4 to 6 in off the chest, and the same guide puts the sticking point at 3 to 4 in; elbow extension demand peaks, and the sticking point typically falls, with the upper arms roughly parallel to the floor [Nu16].
- Not verified: 30% of bar height [E89 via a review]; 24.4% of the ascent [vdT10 via a snippet]; grip-width percentages of 11.4, 17.3, 22.5% [Wagner 1992 via the same review].

What the joints do in it:

- Chart values [L21]: elbow interior angle at the end of the sticking region 86 to 88 deg (about 90 deg of flexion). Chart abduction 71 / 72 / 65 deg. Shoulder flexion falls from about 117 to 119 deg at the chest to 62 to 78 deg at the end of the sticking region (chart). Between the first peak and the end of the sticking region the chart abduction rises by 13 deg (medium) and 10 deg (wide); between the end and the second peak by 9 and 8 deg; chest to first peak by 5 and 4 deg. This is arithmetic on chart readings.
- [E89] abstract: the weight's moment arm about the shoulder falls throughout the ascent; about the elbow it falls early, is at a minimum in the sticking region and then rises. EMG was maximal and flat. The sticking region was not due to a longer moment arm or less muscle activity.
- [McL 2.9]: nearly every missed lift in his films shows the elbows rotated out to the position nearest the plane through shoulders and neck (Figure 12) at the sticking point, no angle; better to rotate the other way, elbows more toward the sides, and all lifters should avoid the fully rotated-out position until lockout. This is the opposite caution to Nuckols' "flare through the midrange"; the two are reconcilable only if "fully rotated out" is an extreme, which no source quantifies.
- Coaching: elbow extension demand is highest, and the sticking point typically falls, with the upper arms roughly parallel to the floor [Nu16].

## 5. What the sources disagree on or do not say

Not found by the researchers at any readable source:

- A measured time series of elbow flare through the descent. So no evidence for or against "tuck on the way down".
- A measured flare series through the ascent in the same angle as [M22].
- Whether skilled lifters flare more or less than novices on the way up.
- Forearm inclination from the side, and at the chest instead of as a whole-rep mean.
- Where the elbow sits relative to the bar from the side, and how far below the shoulder or chest line it drops. Only the rule minimum and the elbow moment arm at the chest exist.
- Any elbow flare, flexion or forearm number for a 0.42 m grip. Nearest: [M22] 112% and [L21] 0.40 m. [K17] measured no joint angles.
- Any measured scapular or glenohumeral movement in the bench press, in the papers the researchers could read. One 2019 paper reportedly measured it (Borges E et al., J Phys Educ Sport 2019;19(Suppl 3):912-916, 15 volunteers: scapular-girdle abduction about 7.8 deg on the barbell bench press against 19.8 deg on a lever chest-press machine). Only search-tool fragments of its abstract were seen; the numbers, the measure and the lifting load are unverified.
- Full texts of [MK23], [GT16], [vdT09], [vdT10], Wagner 1992, Green and Comfort 2007, [MM84], [E89], [DC07], [DC11]: only abstracts or nothing.

Where sources disagree or are unclear:

- Measured flare at the chest versus coaching. Measured abduction for medium and wide grips is 59 and 66 deg [M22]. Nuckols recommends 45 to 60 deg [Nu16] with no study cited. Rippetoe prefers about 75 deg [Ri11]. The measured values are at or above the top of Nuckols' band and below Rippetoe's.
- Does flare change in the rep? Nuckols says tucked at the chest, then flare [Nu16]. Rippetoe's draft gives one humerus angle and no change [Ri11]. [Ki23] says the elbows trace one angle. [McL 2.9] warns against rotating the elbows to the extreme position before the lift is finished. Only the Nuckols claim is a "change in flare"; it has no numbers.
- Two different "abduction" angles. [M22] is humerus against the sagittal plane of the thorax. [L21]'s plane is not defined in the text the researchers read and it rises from 42 to 58 deg at the chest to 72 to 80 deg late in the ascent (L21 does not say why; "the arm becomes vertical" was the researchers' guess and is not supported). The 22 to 30 deg rise is therefore the rise of that angle, not a measured flare increase. The direction (arm abducts as bar rises) is stated in the text of [L21] and [L22] and a rise in "abduction" is confirmed there; the amounts are chart readings.
- Elbow moment arm. [L21] says it rises from the chest to the first peak and falls from the end of the sticking region to the second peak. [E89] says it falls early, is minimal in the sticking region and rises after. Different lifters (recreational against elite), different definitions, [E89] abstract only.
- Forearm: coaches say vertical in all views [Ri11] and vertical at the chest [Le24][Fe13][Ki23]. [M22] measured whole-rep frontal means from -16.5 to +12.5 deg, which depend on grip width. The two are not in conflict if "vertical" means at the chest with a grip chosen for it, but the chest value was not measured.
- Elbow relative to the bar. Nuckols 2015: bar directly above the elbows at all times [Nu15]. Nuckols 2016: elbows 1 to 2 in ahead at the chest and back under by the midrange [Nu16]. [McL 2.6]: moving the bar toward the shoulder moves the elbow away from the bar's line of action, so the elbow ends ahead of the bar.
- Elbow flexion at the chest. [M22] 87 to 101 deg (medium and wide); [L21] interior angle 55 to 68 deg (flexion about 112 to 125, subtraction); [K17] "about 90", cited and untraced; [MK23] 95 and 105 (unverified). The researchers' advice: treat as roughly 90 to 115 deg and state the method. [L21] uses the acromion tip, a touch-and-press 1RM and a different marker model.
- Elbow flexion at lockout. [M22] peak minus ROM leaves about 22 to 26 deg (researcher's subtraction), so the measured window does not end at 0 deg. The rule requires locked elbows [IPF26]. [L21] interior angle at the second peak is 133 to 135 deg, which is not lockout.
- Touch point with a close grip. Nuckols says most people touch lower [Nu16]. [L21] found no difference in horizontal bar distance at the chest between grips. Descent distance is longer for narrow grips ([L21]: 0.399 vs 0.338 m), which fits.
- [L21] bar-to-shoulder reference point. The horizontal column is headed "from start". [L22] uses "from the shoulder origin". [L21] and [McL] agree in size at the chest (12.5 to 13.1 against 13.4 cm) so the sign and reference look right, but this is not confirmed.
- [L22]: bar still 0.090 m from the shoulder at the second peak (28.5 cm up). [L21]: about 0 at 31 to 37 cm up. The bar-path researcher's note says both papers "agree" on a crossing by 28 to 31 cm; the table of [L22] does not show it. [L22] also has the sticking region ending at 0.290 m in its text and a velocity minimum at about 0.18 m in its table. Times differ from [L21] (second peak at about 3.0 s against 1.0 to 1.4 s) and are not reconciled.
- [K17] lift distance: 0.41 vs 0.43 m, text says no significant difference, Table 1 prints p = 0.018 (and p = 0.044 for duration). Not a contradiction: the paper's alpha is 0.01 (abstract, Table 1 footnote), so neither is significant. Treat the 2 cm as small.
- Resultant force angle at the end of the sticking region in [L21] (84.4, 88.7, 98.8 deg): the angle's reference is not in the notes. The step from bar force direction to forearm tilt is an inference.
- Raising times in [McL] Table 3 come from a scan on a blog; the text copy has no table. The book, not the 1984 papers, is the source of all [McL] numbers; the journal abstract of [MM84] has no centimetre values. The blog is a transcription, not the print book.
- [McL 2.9] gives the direction of the "maximum" elbow rotation in words and in the Figure 12 caption (elbows nearest the plane through the shoulders and neck, "rotate themselves out"; the alternative is "elbows more towards sides"). It gives no angles, and calls three-dimensional studies of the arm angle still to come.

Which numbers come from coaching and not from measurement:

- All side-view forearm statements, "forearm vertical". [Ri11][Le24][Fe13][Ki23][Ri18].
- 45 to 60 deg [Nu16]; 75, 45 to 75, 90 deg [Ri11]; 25 to 30% lateral bar force [Nu16], no source named.
- Elbows 1 to 2 in ahead of the bar [Nu16]; bar 1 to 3 in below the over-shoulder line at the touch [Ki23].
- Descent of 2 to 3 s [Nu16]; stuck at 4 to 6 in, or 3 to 4 in, in the same guide [Nu16]. The competition pause "on the average lasts about two seconds" is McLaughlin's own estimate, not a measurement [McL 2.12].
- Touch between the nipple and the lower sternum, lower with a close grip, wide grip higher [Nu16]; mid-sternum [Le24].
- Close grip within 90% of the normal bench; 32 in (81 cm) grip gives under 2 in of travel for some lightweight women [Ri18].

Other limits:

- [M22] mixes powerlifters and recreational lifters, n = 35, six to eight reps, grips defined by arm-to-bar angle. [L21] is 14 recreational men, 1RM, touch-and-press. [McL] is 2D film from the side (world-class lifters of 1978 to 1980), so it cannot see elbow flare. The grid mixes these protocols.
- [N24] sets abduction at 45, 70 and 90 deg with a 16 kg bar and reports forces only. It brackets what lifters can hold, not what they do.
- Skill difference in flare: no usable source. The only number is an unverified search-tool snippet of [MK23] (58.4 vs 60.3 deg), whose group labels are doubtful (section 2.2). The [MK23] abstract itself says powerlifters had shorter joint ROMs and a different bar path.

### 6.1 What the model does now

Changed on 2026-11-24 in `src/lib/lift-motion.ts` and `src/lib/lift-motion-tables.ts`; the numbers are the model's, tested in `tests/helpers/lift-motion/bench.ts`. Pairs are bench / close grip.

The rule. Seen from the side the elbows stay under the bar, out to the sides of it. As the bar nears the chest they come a little toward the feet of it: 1 cm for the bench, 6 cm for the close grip (the `lead` of each lift). On the way up the bar returns toward the head earlier than it left, and the elbows follow half of that early return (`elbowLag`). Everything else follows from the lengths of the arm: the flare, the elbow's bend, how far the elbows are outside the hands.

The set-up changed with it, because the old one could not hold the measured numbers:

- The shoulder joints lie 4.5 cm below the chest point, pulled back onto the pad. That the shoulder blades are retracted is [Ri11] and [Nu16]; neither gives a distance. The 4.5 cm is a judgement: with the next two items it makes the bar's travel the measured one.
- The bar lies on the heel of the palm, 5.5 cm above the wrist, and rests on the drawn chest instead of sinking 2.8 cm into it.
- The bar then travels 0.40 m (close grip 0.43 m). Before it travelled 0.50 m; lifters were measured at 0.41 and 0.43 m [K17] and 0.34 to 0.40 m [L21].
- The hands are 0.76 m apart, which on this mannequin (shoulder joints 0.38 m apart, the middle of a hand 4.5 cm outside its forefinger) is 175% of the shoulder width between the forefingers, the grip trained lifters chose in [K17]. From the shoulder joint to the middle of the hand its arm-to-bar angle at lockout is 72 deg, between the medium (75) and wide (65) grips of [M22]. The close grip is 0.50 m, 105% of the shoulder width between the forefingers, among the narrow grips of [K17] (95%), [L21] (100%) and [M22] (112%); arm-to-bar 85 deg, as the narrow grip of [M22] (85). A first version had the close hands at 0.47 m and the elbows 5 cm ahead of the bar; at the chest its forearms leaned in 35 deg and read as elbows flared wide. Why T9 is not met. With the hands over the shoulders, the elbow of a two-bone arm is about 0.2 m away from the line between shoulder and wrist at mid-height, whatever the swivel. That distance has to go somewhere: out to the side (the forearm leans in, seen from the front) or toward the feet (the forearm leans back, seen from the side). The model puts most of it out to the side, because [L21] read lifters' elbows as 65 to 72 deg open half way up (on its chart of unstated plane), because every coach asks for the bar over the elbows seen from the side, and because the earlier model, which put it toward the feet, looked wrong to a lifter. A lifter with a higher chest has less of that distance to place: the elbow bends less. [M22] does not say which way its forearm angle is signed, nor where in the rep the forearm leans most.

Shoulder girdle, what the sources say (question 7):

- No source the researchers could read measured scapular or glenohumeral motion during a bench press (one 2019 paper reportedly did; unverified, section 5). [M22] estimates the glenohumeral centre by regression from skin markers (Rab et al.); [L21] and [L22] use an acromion marker. Skin markers cannot see the scapula slide.
- Rippetoe: the scapulae are pinned against the bench and "do not move if the position is assumed correctly"; "think of it as if the elbows move and the shoulders do not (even though this is not literally true)" [Ri11]. Arch rotates the shoulder joint back under the bar and shortens the bar path [Ri11].
- Nuckols: retraction reduces range of motion by pushing the chest up; leg drive pushes the chest higher and back into the bar [Nu16]. No distances.
- Arch angle (sternum vs horizontal) from [M22]: 32.3 (wide), 29.9 (medium), 28.0 (narrow, elbows out), 25.8 deg (narrow, elbows in).
- [L21] suggests the scapula moves laterally and up after the sticking region. Their inference, no scapular data.
- [N24] models retracted, neutral and released scapulae and reports forces only.
